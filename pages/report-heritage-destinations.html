Heritage Destinations: The gate and the night

Why heritage sites draw record crowds while the land around them keeps little of what visitors spend, and what that land should hold, with a view from Egypt, Saudi Arabia and the wider region.

In brief

The great heritage sites now ration and price their gates with care. Most of what their visitors spend still lands outside the gate, where planning is weakest.

This report looks at 28 heritage sites and museums, from Giza and Petra to Pompeii and Angkor, and asks where a visitor's money goes. The ticket matters less than four other things: where the visitor arrives, how late the site stays open, whether the ticket rewards a night, and who plans the land in between. It then applies the evidence to Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Jordan and the wider region.

  1. The gate keeps a few per cent of what a visitor spends.

    A foreign adult ticket to the Grand Egyptian Museum costs about US$30, roughly 3% of the US$975 the average international visitor spent in Egypt in 2024. Angkor earned about US$44 per foreign visitor in 2017, around 7% of Cambodia's receipts per arrival.

  2. Price has rarely held visitors back.

    When Angkor raised its one-day pass 85% in 2017, foreign visitors still grew 11.8% and ticket revenue 72.5%, although tourism businesses reported that visitors were shortening their trips. The Louvre lifted its full price 29% in 2024, lost 2% of visitors, then recovered.

  3. Sites are shortening the visit by design.

    Pompeii, the Acropolis and the Grand Egyptian Museum each admit up to 20,000 a day. The Colosseum allows 75 minutes inside, Pompeii sells 75% of its summer capacity before 13:00, and nine of the 13 benchmark sites that publish hours close by 19:00.

  4. Arrival points decide where the money lands.

    Car parks, ticket halls and shuttles have moved off the monuments, to a new highway entrance at Giza built for 1,200 cars and 300 buses and to a visitor centre 2.4 km away at Stonehenge, and the commercial plots have moved with them. Buffer zones vary a thousandfold, and size has not decided outcomes: Liverpool lost its listing with a buffer 5.5 times its core.

  5. A few sites price the night.

    Petra charges JD 90 to a visitor who does not sleep in Jordan and JD 50 to one who does, with a second day for JD 5. Machu Picchu sells 1,000 tickets a day only in its gateway district, and Diriyah's ticket can be spent in Bujairi's restaurants.

  6. The region is running two opposite experiments.

    Egypt has invested at the gate and in arrival: the Grand Egyptian Museum and a new Giza entrance opened in 2025, a year of a record 19 million arrivals and 18.6 million visits to its sites and museums. Saudi Arabia has wrapped a 28.78 ha core in a 14 km² district at Diriyah and placed Hegra under a 22,561 km² planning authority, while AlUla's rooms and visitors remain below earlier targets.

What it means

Recommendations by audience

Governments and land agencies

Define and zone the land around each core before the next investment arrives, turn the foreign premium into a reward for staying, and publish visits and nights by site.

Master developers

Treat the arrival point as the first plot, open the evening before the hotels, phase rooms on measured nights and design for domestic visitors.

Investors and lenders

Underwrite nights per visit, stress-test single-site gateways for a 60% fall in a year, and read the concession before the plot.

Site operators

Use slots and multi-day passes to send time into the district, open later and make the ticket spendable outside the gate.

Frequently asked questions

How much of a visitor's spending does a heritage site keep?

Very little: at three flagship sites the ticket equals about 2% to 7% of what the average international visitor spends in the country. A Grand Egyptian Museum ticket is about 3% of what the average international visitor spent in Egypt in 2024, and Angkor's ticket revenue per foreign visitor was about 7% of Cambodia's receipts per arrival.

Do higher ticket prices reduce visitor numbers at heritage sites?

Rarely. When Angkor raised its one-day pass by 85% in 2017, foreign visitors still grew 11.8% and ticket revenue rose 72.5%, although tourism businesses reported shorter trips.

How can heritage destinations capture more visitor spending?

By pricing the night and planning the land outside the gate, as Petra does by charging JD 90 to a foreign day visitor and JD 50 to one who sleeps in Jordan. Sites can also open into the evening and treat the arrival point as the first plot of a gateway district.
